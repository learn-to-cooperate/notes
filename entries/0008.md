# Cooperation in Crises

**Date:** 2026-02-06

## Wir sitzen alle im selben Boot… aber nicht gleich

We are all in the same boat… but not equally.

## Learning to cooperate (notes toward something real)

> Cooperation begins not in the comfort of 'we', but in noticing who was never allowed to say it.

## The unease beneath the chant

> The 'we' is never neutral: it is a sorting mechanism disguised as an embrace.

In the communal sphere, the performative utterance of “together we are stronger” (or “one,” “better,” “unstoppable”) stirs a quiet unease. It poses as a shared spirit of unity, yet the “we” quietly functions as a boundary drawn in the shadows—relegating the unassimilated, those overlooked in liminal zones of disregard, tucked into the algorithmic oubliettes of .gitignore, or already carried into silence—to the unseen underside where their absence silently props up the collective image. This easy chant papers over the exclusions it relies on, leaving the fragile edifice of purported synergy feeling hollow and precarious.

## Cooperation in case of emergency: the boat that isn't quite shared

> "'Wir sitzen alle im selben Boot' sounds like solidarity, but often whispers: 'If only I had a better boat.'"

When people invoke "Wir sitzen alle im selben Boot" (we're all in the same boat) in a crisis—pandemic, climate storm, economic crash—it rarely lands as pure solidarity. The phrase conjures one vessel riding the same waves, fate binding everyone equally. But listen closer: the tone often carries a quiet edge, a resigned shrug that says "no one escapes this mess," more than "let's row together."

It feels less like an invitation to mutual aid and more like reluctant acceptance of shared doom. 

Beneath the surface, there's frequently an unspoken wish: if only I had a faster boat, a yacht, a private raft with better oars, more life vests, or even a helicopter overhead. The "same boat" rhetoric flattens the deck for a moment, but it doesn't redistribute the seats. Some are still in steerage, bailing water with bare hands, while others have cabins, satellite phones, and escape plans.

The performative claim of unity in emergency papers over the tiers: who gets the first lifeboat, who clings to debris, who was never allowed aboard to begin with. It can sound like cooperation, but it often masks a deeper individualism—if we truly believed we were in the same boat, the distribution of safety would shift. Instead, the phrase sometimes serves to justify inaction: "We're all suffering equally, so why rock the boat?"

> Unity declared in crisis rarely redistributes the lifeboats.

True emergency cooperation would admit the hull is uneven, the weight distributed unfairly, and start reallocating ropes, buckets, and turns at the helm. Until then, "Wir sitzen alle im selben Boot" drifts like a half-hearted flare—visible, but not quite lighting the way for everyone. This leaves the fragile edifice of purported solidarity feeling hollow and precarious—yet perhaps that's exactly where real learning to cooperate begins.

## Where real cooperation actually begins
 
> Real cooperation begins in the crack where the slogan rings hollow and the comforting rhythm of 'we' quietly draws its own boundaries.

Life's not fair, and there will never be enough lifeboats. Cooperation in scarcity isn't about achieving perfect equality or universal rescue—it's about refusing to let the unfairness become total indifference. It's about small acts that make the difference between chaos and something resembling dignity: giving up your spot when it matters, listening to the ones below deck, reallocating what little there is so fewer drown alone. Those acts rarely "fix" the boat, but they change the quality of the time left, the way people face the waves, and sometimes—quietly—they save more lives than anyone expected.

In the spirit of these notes: the chant of "Wir sitzen alle im selben Boot" is often hollow or self-serving, but the response isn't to abandon ship entirely. It's to row harder together, eyes open to the tilt, knowing the lifeboats are insufficient, yet insisting that shared effort is still the only thing that keeps the human part of us afloat a little longer.
